### R01 · Field Notes №01 · THE PITCH
Image: research/01-the-pitch.png
Source: Sydney Brenner · letter to Max Perutz · 1963

In 1963 Sydney Brenner wrote a letter asking for something new: an animal simple enough that we could understand its whole nervous system.

Soon after, he settled on a transparent nematode, 1 mm long: C. elegans.

60 years later, Spoor feeds that nematode's wiring a market.

### R02 · Field Notes №02 · THE MAP
Image: research/02-the-map.png
Source: White, Southgate, Thomson & Brenner · Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B · 1986

1986: White, Southgate, Thomson and Brenner publish a 340-page paper.

Thousands of electron microscope slices, traced by hand, became the first complete wiring diagram of an animal's nervous system.

The subtitle called it the animal's mind.

### R03 · Field Notes №03 · THE COUNT
Image: research/03-the-count.png
Source: White et al. · 1986

Your brain: ~86 billion neurons.
A fruit fly: ~140,000.
C. elegans: 302.

Small enough to simulate every single one on a laptop.

That's what Spoor does every minute.

### R04 · Field Notes №04 · THE LINEAGE
Image: research/04-the-lineage.png
Source: Sulston et al. · Developmental Biology · 1983

John Sulston watched C. elegans embryos under a microscope for hours, cell by cell, until he had the full family tree.

959 cells in the adult. The same tree in every animal.

This is why C. elegans is the most mapped animal on Earth.

### R10 · Field Notes №10 · THE SMELL
Image: research/10-the-smell.png
Source: Bargmann, Hartwieg & Horvitz · Cell · 1993

C. elegans has no nose. A handful of sensory neurons, AWA and AWC among them, pick up a long list of odours.

Kill one with a laser and the animal stops responding to its smells.

In Spoor, the market's scent goes into exactly these neurons.

### R11 · Field Notes №11 · THE HUNT
Image: research/11-the-hunt.png
Source: Pierce-Shimomura, Morse & Lockery · J. Neurosci. · 1999

How does a nematode find food with no map and no plan?

When the smell gets weaker, it turns more often. When it gets stronger, it keeps going.

Pierce-Shimomura et al., 1999. The "pirouette" strategy.

Spoor crawls across 11 markets the same way.

### R13 · Field Notes №13 · FORWARD / BACK
Image: research/13-forward-back.png
Source: Chalfie et al. · J. Neurosci. · 1985

Touch a nematode's nose, it backs up. Touch its tail, it goes forward.

Chalfie et al. (1985) killed neurons one by one with a laser and found the command cells: AVA for reverse, AVB for forward.

In Spoor, AVA against AVB is literally strike or step back.

### R14 · Field Notes №14 · THE CIRCUIT
Image: research/14-the-circuit.png
Source: Gray, Hill & Bargmann · PNAS · 2005

Gray, Hill & Bargmann (2005) mapped the navigation circuit:
AIY pushes the animal to keep going.
AIB and AIZ push it to turn.
RIM shapes the reversals.

Every one of these is live in the Spoor lab. Watch them light up.

### R15 · Field Notes №15 · THE SIGN
Image: research/15-the-sign.png
Source: Chalasani et al. · Nature · 2007

A wiring diagram tells you who's connected. Not whether a connection excites or inhibits.

Chalasani et al. (2007) showed AWC inhibits AIY with glutamate.

We hand-set that sign and others from the literature. Without them, the simulated brain either seized or went silent.

### R05 · Field Notes №05 · THE NOBEL

2002 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine: Brenner, Horvitz and Sulston.

### R06 · Field Notes №06 · CELL DEATH
Image: research/06-cell-death.png
Source: Ellis & Horvitz · Cell · 1986

As a C. elegans hermaphrodite develops, 131 cells die on schedule. Every time, the same ones.

Studying them, Robert Horvitz found the genes for programmed cell death, which also run in us.

Good traders cut what doesn't work. So does Spoor.

### R07 · Field Notes №07 · THE GENOME
Image: research/07-the-genome.png
Source: C. elegans Sequencing Consortium · Science · 1998

1998: C. elegans became the first multicellular animal to have its whole genome read.

About 20,000 genes, roughly as many as you have.

A brain that's tiny, but not simple.

### R08 · Field Notes №08 · RNA INTERFERENCE
Image: research/08-rna-interference.png
Source: Fire, Mello et al. · Nature · 1998

In 1998 Andrew Fire and Craig Mello injected nematodes with double-stranded RNA and switched specific genes off.

RNA interference. Nobel Prize, 2006.

C. elegans keeps giving science its tools.

### R09 · Field Notes №09 · GLOW
Image: research/09-glow.png
Source: Chalfie et al. · Science · 1994 · Nobel Prize 2008

1994: Martin Chalfie put a jellyfish gene for green fluorescent protein into C. elegans. Its touch neurons glowed.

Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 2008.

Green still marks the forward side of the brain in Spoor.

### R12 · Field Notes №12 · THE STEER
Image: research/12-the-steer.png
Source: Iino & Yoshida · J. Neurosci. · 2009

Nematodes don't only pirouette. They also curve slowly toward a stronger smell as they crawl, like a weathervane.

Iino & Yoshida, 2009.

Two strategies, no brain bigger than 302 cells.

### R17 · Field Notes №17 · HABITUATION
Image: research/17-habituation.png
Source: Rankin, Beck & Chiba · Behav. Brain Res. · 1990

Tap a nematode's dish and it backs up. Tap it again and again, and it stops caring.

Catharine Rankin's lab showed nematodes habituate, and can remember it for 24 hours.

Spoor forgets faster: its market memory halves every 6 hours.

### R18 · Field Notes №18 · LONGEVITY
Image: research/18-longevity.png
Source: Kenyon et al. · Nature · 1993

Cynthia Kenyon changed one gene, daf-2, and the animals lived twice as long.

1993. It started the modern science of ageing.

Spoor is aiming for something smaller: surviving the market without blowing up the book.

### R19 · Field Notes №19 · SLEEP
Image: research/19-sleep.png
Source: Raizen et al. · Nature · 2008

Before each molt, C. elegans goes quiet in a sleep-like state called lethargus.

Raizen et al., 2008: even 302 neurons need sleep.

Spoor doesn't. It sniffs every 60 seconds, 24/7.

### R20 · Field Notes №20 · SPACE
Image: research/20-space.png
Source: NASA · 2003

In 2003 the space shuttle Columbia broke up on re-entry. Canisters of C. elegans on board were found in the debris weeks later.

The nematodes were alive.

Tough animal. Good brain to build on.

### R21 · Field Notes №21 · WHOLE-BRAIN
Image: research/21-whole-brain.png
Source: Schrödel et al. · Nature Methods · 2013 · Kato et al. · Cell · 2015

2013-2015: researchers imaged nearly every neuron in a living nematode's head at the same time.

Kato et al. (2015) found the whole brain moving through a few shared patterns: forward, turn, reverse.

That's the same three-way choice Spoor makes each minute.

### R22 · Field Notes №22 · THE SPIKE
Image: research/22-the-spike.png
Source: Liu et al. · Cell · 2018

Honest science post: most C. elegans neurons don't fire classic spikes. They use graded voltages.

Liu et al. (2018) found an exception: AWA, a smell neuron, fires real action potentials.

Spoor uses a spiking model for all 302. It's a model, not an animal.

### R24 · Field Notes №24 · GROWING UP
Image: research/24-growing-up.png
Source: Witvliet et al. · Nature · 2021

Witvliet et al. (2021) mapped the brains of 8 nematodes at different ages, birth to adult.

The core layout stays. The connections keep growing and changing.

A brain is a moving target. So is a market.

### R25 · Field Notes №25 · THE WIRELESS LAYER
Image: research/25-the-wireless-layer.png
Source: Ripoll-Sánchez et al. · Neuron · 2023

The wiring diagram isn't the whole story.

Ripoll-Sánchez et al. (2023) mapped a "wireless" network: neuropeptides passing signals between neurons that never touch.

Spoor doesn't model it yet. Saying so up front.

### R26 · Field Notes №26 · THE ATLAS
Image: research/26-the-atlas.png
Source: Randi, Sharma, Dvali & Leifer · Nature · 2023

Randi et al. (2023) poked C. elegans neurons one at a time and recorded what responded.

Plenty of signals didn't follow the wiring diagram.

So no, Spoor isn't a digital nematode. It's what you get from the map alone. The four books show how far that goes.

### R27 · Field Notes №27 · THE ROBOT
Image: research/27-the-robot.png
Source: OpenWorm community · Timothy Busbice · 2014

2014: Timothy Busbice loaded the C. elegans connectome into a Lego robot. A sonar fed the "nose" neurons, motor neurons drove the wheels.

It wandered and backed away from walls, with no rules written for either.

Spoor swapped the sonar for a market.

### R29 · Field Notes №29 · THE METHOD
Image: research/29-the-method.png
Source: Shiu et al. · Nature · 2024

Shiu et al. (2024) built a leaky integrate-and-fire model straight from the fly connectome, and it predicted which neurons drive feeding.

Same idea as ours: real wiring, simple spiking neurons, see what comes out.

Spoor = that method, 302 neurons, a market for food.
